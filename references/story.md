# Story and content

How to judge whether a talk's story is strong, independent of how the slides look. A weak story
cannot be rescued by slide design. Run this twice: **early**, on the headlines-only storyboard,
when fixing the story is cheap; and **at the end**, on the built deck and speaker notes.

Evidence key: **✓✓** replicated research · **✓** strong agreement among practitioners ·
**⚠** nuance, or the experts disagree.

## 1. One throughline ✓

A strong talk is built around one central idea: Anderson's *throughline*, Duarte's *big idea*,
the Heaths' *Simple*. The speaker can say it in one sentence of at most 15 words.

- **Test:** write the message in 15 words or fewer. If it will not fit, or there are several
  competing messages, the talk has no throughline yet.
- **⚠ Nuance:** Anderson allows two or three ideas if one throughline connects them. It is a
  spine, not a single lesson.
- **Use it to cut.** Every section earns its place by supporting the throughline; the rest goes.
  Anderson's point that a talk's strength often lies in what is left out holds for every slot
  length.

## 2. The audience is the hero ✓

- **Cast the audience as the hero and the speaker as the guide**, not the speaker, their company
  or their product. Ask: *who are they when they leave the room?* Credit wins to "we" and "you",
  and let the room feel it is succeeding.
- **The idea only needs to be new to this audience**, not to the world. Start where they are,
  match the technical depth to what they know, and say early why it matters to them.
- **The enemy is the curse of knowledge** ✓✓ (Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber 1989; Heath & Heath,
  *Made to Stick*). Once the speaker knows something, they cannot imagine not knowing it, and
  their explanations go opaque. The remedy is to try the talk on someone outside their circle.
- **Six failure modes to check**, slide by slide: ✓
  1. **Jargon:** terms or acronyms a real share of the room does not know.
  2. **Pace:** rushing through the hard part.
  3. **Abstraction:** a concept with no concrete example.
  4. **Skipped steps:** a premise or link the expert fills in without noticing.
  5. **Missing context:** no connection to why the topic matters.
  6. **Assumed buy-in:** taking for granted that the room already cares.

## 3. Structure: contrast creates momentum ✓

A flat list ("and… and… and…") loses the room. Pick a structure with tension in it:

- **What is ↔ what could be** (Duarte, *Resonate*): open with the shared present, move back and
  forth between the present and the better future several times, and close on the world after
  the idea is adopted, with the next step. ⚠ The middle alternates many times; it is not one
  linear "what could be".
- **And, But, Therefore** (Olson, *Houston, We Have a Narrative*): setup, problem, resolution.
  Suits technical and data-heavy talks; it turns a list of components into a problem and its
  answer.
- **Problem → solution → proof → next step**, the default for technical talks.
- **What / so what / now what:** never a fact without its consequence.
- **Situation, Complication, Question, Answer** (Minto, *The Pyramid Principle*) for rooms that
  want the answer first. ⚠ Answer-first suits decisions; teaching usually needs the learner to
  earn the answer.
- **⚠ Do not force a full hero's journey** onto a short technical talk; it overcomplicates.

**Teaching one point well.** For a teaching talk or a workshop block, depth beats coverage:
teach one point properly rather than five in passing, and pick the point where the audience is
most likely making a costly mistake. Frame it as a question the audience is already asking, show
the mistake and what it costs, then show what to do instead and how, with one real example. The
speaker asks the questions aloud and answers them, which keeps the talk conversational.
(Inspired by Andy Harrington's teaching material; see `README.md`.)

**Test:** is there visible tension and a build, or a flat list of facts?

## 4. Stories carry the numbers ✓ / ⚠

People remember stories far better than statistics (Heath & Heath, *Made to Stick*) ✓. A number
on its own is forgotten; a number tied to something the audience can picture stays.

- **The speaker keeps two or three core stories ready:** usually a struggle and a turning point,
  rehearsed until they are reliable.
- **Make numbers relatable.** Re-scale a figure into something the body can feel: not "the
  service handles 2 million requests a day" but "every person in Paris, once a day". On the
  slide the number is the evidence; the comparison is what people keep (`slides.md` §3).
- **Tell the same story differently for different rooms:** a leadership room wants the
  strategy, a team wants the execution, customers want the value.
- **Test:** does each key number ride on a story or a comparison?

## 5. One next step ✓

- **Exactly one concrete ask** at the end: one action people can take this week. People act when
  the action feels small and doable.
- **The companion guide shows how.** Its closing "Your next step" block repeats the action and
  explains how to do it; the last slide shows the guide's QR code.
- **The speaker promises the guide in the first minute**, so the room knows it can listen instead
  of taking notes.
- **Test:** is there exactly one ask, not several competing or vague ones? ⚠ There is no
  controlled study comparing one ask with several; one ask is consensus craft advice.

## 6. Order: first and last matter most ✓✓

People recall the first and last items of a sequence best and the middle worst (Murdock 1962;
Glanzer & Cunitz 1966). So:

- **The speaker scripts the first minute and the last lines.** They matter most.
- **Open with a hook**: a question, a surprising number, a short story, a vivid problem. Not an
  agenda slide. A question most of the room will answer "yes" to pulls them in at once.
- **Put the strongest material at the start and the end**; do not bury the key point in the
  middle.
- **Close by zooming out** to what the idea makes possible. A callback to the opening helps
  recall and gives a sense of completion.
- **⚠ Nuance:** the advantage of the last items fades within seconds once something else comes
  along, while the first items reach long-term memory. For lasting recall, the opening is worth
  more than the close.
- **Signpost** the transitions so the room always knows where it is.

## 7. Cut to the essentials ✓

- Group and prioritise; three points beat seven. Every point supports the throughline.
- Lead with the most important thing in each section; do not bury it (`concision.md`).

## Story check, early (on the storyboard)

Measure the storyboard against the brief (`talk-brief.md`).

- [ ] The structure fits the brief's goal (teach → clear structure; persuade → contrast; …).
- [ ] The throughline fits one sentence of at most 15 words. Write it out.
- [ ] There is one throughline, and every slide supports it. Name the slides that do not.
- [ ] The talk starts where this audience is; depth matches their knowledge.
- [ ] Why it matters to them is clear early.
- [ ] The audience is the hero, not the speaker or the product.
- [ ] Curse-of-knowledge scan: jargon, pace, abstraction, skipped steps, missing context,
      assumed buy-in.
- [ ] A recognisable structure with tension and a build, not a flat list.
- [ ] A hook in the first minute, not an agenda.
- [ ] Strongest material at the start and the end.
- [ ] A close that zooms out, ideally with a callback.
- [ ] Exactly one next step, concrete, at the end; the guide is promised early.

**Output:** the throughline as you read it, the top three to five story fixes (reorder, cut,
add, sharpen the hook or the next step), and any risk to audience fit. The speaker confirms the
revised storyboard before anything is built.

## Story check, final (on the built deck and notes)

- [ ] The throughline is still clear in the finished deck.
- [ ] The one next step is there, concrete, at the end, and matches the guide.
- [ ] The first minute and the last lines are scripted in the speaker notes.
- [ ] The final slide order still tells the strongest version of the story.
- [ ] No spelling or grammar errors in slides, notes, captions or alt text.
- [ ] One word for one thing throughout: list every variant found and pick one form.
- [ ] Names, capitalisation, numbers, units and dates are written the same way everywhere.
- [ ] Acronyms are spelled out on first use.

**Output:** every fix made, every term standardised (term → chosen form), and confirmation that
the throughline and the next step survived the build.
