# The talk brief

The talk brief comes before the storyboard, before the first slide and before any image. It
fixes what the talk is for, and everything built later is measured against it: the story
critique, the slide build, the final story pass and the rehearsal.

Write it with the speaker, not for them. Ask the open questions in plain words, offer a
recommendation where a choice is categorical, and let the speaker edit the result. If an
answer is vague, ask once more for the concrete version; do not fill the gap with a guess.

## 1. The goal: what changes for the audience

A talk moves the audience from one state to another (Duarte, *Resonate*). The goal decides the
structure, so the speaker picks one primary goal even if the talk touches several:

| Goal | They leave… | Structure that fits (see `story.md` §3) |
|---|---|---|
| Teach | knowing something | clear structure: what / so what / now what |
| Persuade | believing something | contrast between what is and what could be |
| Inspire | feeling something | story-led, with a turning point |
| Drive action | doing something | problem → solution → proof → next step |

## 2. The one desired outcome, and the one next step

Ask: *what should the audience do, think or feel differently afterwards?* Push for one answer.
If the speaker gives three, ask which one matters most and keep the others as supporting
points.

Turn that outcome into **one next step**: one action people can take this week, small enough
that they will. It is the talk's single call to action, the last thing the speaker asks for,
and the closing section of the companion guide (`story.md` §4).

## 3. The throughline

From the goal and the outcome, draft the throughline: the talk's core message in **one
sentence of at most 15 words** (Anderson, *TED Talks*). Confirm it with the speaker now. If it
will not fit in one sentence, the brief is not ready, and no slide will fix that.

## 4. The audience

The audience is the hero of the talk (`story.md` §2). The speaker should find out, not guess:
ask the organiser, look at who came to past editions, and if possible talk to two or three
people who will be in the room (`delivery.md` §1). Capture:

- who they are: roles, seniority, background
- how many: a handful, dozens or hundreds
- what they already know about the topic, and how technical they are
- what they care about and what problem they bring
- whether they have seen similar talks, and how they will arrive: curious, sceptical, or
  there because someone sent them

## 5. Format, length and room

### Talk or workshop

Ask explicitly; it changes the whole plan.

- **Talk or keynote:** one linear story, one throughline, roughly one slide per minute, little
  interaction.
- **Workshop:** modular blocks, each with its own short arc; hands-on exercises; checkpoints
  where stragglers catch up; a minute-budgeted plan with a cut order for a hard stop. The
  throughline becomes the learning goal. Note whether participants work on their own laptops,
  and which operating systems to expect.

Note the interaction too: questions, polls, live demos.

### Length

The slot length sets the slide count and how hard to cut. For a talk, about one slide per
minute. For a workshop, budget minutes per block including the exercises, not per slide, and
decide in advance which block goes first when time runs out.

### Room

The room decides type size and colour scheme (`slides.md` §5, `accessibility.md` §2):

- distance from the screen to the back row
- light: bright or daylit (a light scheme holds up better) or dimmable (a dark scheme works)
- screen shape (16:9 unless told otherwise), projector or LED wall, lectern or open stage
- the slot: time of day, who speaks before and after, whether there is Q&A

## 6. Look and constraints

- The speaker's **style file**, if one exists (look, colours, image kind, motif, fonts). If not,
  the style interview writes one before the build.
- Hard constraints: mandatory slides from the organiser, a language, a template the event
  requires.
- Where the companion guide will live, so its address and QR code can go on the last slide.

## The brief

Write it as a short block, confirm it with the speaker, and keep it next to the talk:

```
BRIEF
Goal:         teach | persuade | inspire | drive action   (primary)
Outcome:      the one thing they do, think or feel differently
Next step:    one action this week
Throughline:  one sentence, at most 15 words
Audience:     who / how many / prior knowledge / what they care about / how they arrive
Format:       talk | workshop · interaction · length → about N slides
Room:         depth to back row / light / screen
Style:        style file, or "interview first"
Guide:        where the companion guide will be published
```

The brief is the contract. The story critique checks the storyboard against it, the final pass
checks that the finished deck still delivers it, and the rehearsal checks that the room would
take the next step.
