# Concision: make the point and stop

Most speakers who know their subject well say too much. This text is the case for saying less
and the checks that catch over-explaining in the slides, the speaker notes and the rehearsal.
Evidence key: **✓✓** peer-reviewed research · **✓** an established framework or credible
source · **◐** a plausible craft cue, not verified.

## Why saying less wins

- **Easy-to-process messages persuade more.** Messages that are easier to process are judged
  more positively and are more persuasive (Bullock, Shulman & Huskey 2021; Dechêne et al. 2010,
  meta-analysis). Cutting clutter makes a message easier to process. ✓✓
- **Over-explaining overloads the listener.** Past a point, cognitive load stops even capable
  people from processing (Sweller, cognitive load theory). One idea at a time. ✓✓
  *Boundary:* the expertise-reversal effect. Small steps help newcomers and can bore experts, so
  apply "one point at a time" hardest for general audiences and relax it for expert rooms.
- **The driver is the curse of expertise.** Experts cannot recall not knowing, so they dive into
  detail and talk over heads, and expertise inflates confidence in their own explanations
  (Fisher & Keil 2016). ✓✓
- **Over-explaining is often protective**: the speaker adds context to head off being
  misunderstood or rejected. Naming the habit in a rehearsal review helps the speaker notice it.
  ◐

Do not claim what failed verification: that hedging words reliably lower perceived competence,
or that working memory holds a fixed "seven plus or minus two" items. Be concise because it
makes the message easier to process and lighter to carry, not for those reasons.

## Ways to get to the point

- **Answer first** (Minto, *The Pyramid Principle*): the conclusion, then the two or three
  reasons, then the detail, and the detail only when someone asks. It reverses the urge to build
  up to the point. Situation, Complication, Question, Answer is the matching opening. ✓
  ⚠ For decisions and answers to questions; a teaching talk often lets the room earn the answer
  (`story.md` §3).
- **Keep a spoken answer short enough to fit a social post**, then stop and let the silence
  work. If the room wants more, it will ask. ◐
- **Plan an explanation for impatient listeners:** background, relevance, the information, the
  ending, then the follow-up (McCormack, *Brief*). ✓
- **One idea per slide**, stated as one sentence (`slides.md` §2). ✓
- **Shorter, slower, stronger:** cut the excess, slow down at the key moments, deliver the point
  with conviction. ◐
- **Pause instead of filling.** Replace "um", "so", "basically" with silence; a pause brakes the
  momentum of over-talking (Schwartzberg, *Get to the Point!*). ✓
- Self-checks worth offering, labelled as unverified ◐: ask "why am I still talking?",
  time-box each section, signpost the transitions, answer in one breath.

## What to flag

On slides and speaker notes:

- a point **made more than once**, or the same idea in the sentence, the image and the notes
- **more than one point** on a slide; slide text longer than two lines
- **explaining what this audience already knows**
- speaker notes written as **full sentences to read** instead of cues
- **a wind-up before the point** ("before I get into this, some background…")
- a section or the whole talk **over its time budget** from the brief

In a rehearsal recording, also: the time from starting an answer to stating its point, and
points restated in other words.

## Fixes

- Move the conclusion to the front and cut the wind-up.
- Collapse each slide to one sentence and one image; push detail into the notes or the
  companion guide.
- Rewrite speaker notes as cues (`slides.md` §7).
- Delete the second statement of a point; trust the room to get it once.
- Mark pauses in the notes; script only the opening and the close.
