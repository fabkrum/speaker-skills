# Delivery and Q&A

The deck is half the talk; the speaker delivers the rest. This text covers sizing up the
audience before the talk, reading the room during it, voice and body, the first minute, what to
do when things go wrong, and questions. Evidence key: **✓** research or strong consensus ·
**◐** craft cue, not verified.

Several techniques here are inspired by TJ Walker's course on sizing up an audience and by Andy
Harrington's delivery material, rewritten in this project's own words (see `README.md`).

## 1. Before the talk: find out who is coming ◐

Do not guess the audience; ask. The speaker emails or calls the organiser and, if possible, two
or three people who will attend, and asks about:

- **who they are**: ages, jobs, backgrounds
- **what they hope to get** from the session, and which topics matter most to them
- **the problems they have** around the topic
- **what they have seen before**: similar talks, what they liked and what bored them
- **what the organiser wants** the session to achieve

The answers feed the brief (`talk-brief.md` §4) and the curse-of-knowledge scan (`story.md` §2):
they set the jargon, the depth and the examples for the people actually in the room.

## 2. During the talk: read the room ◐

- **Watch faces.** Nods and smiles, or frowns and phones? Adjust the pace or the depth.
- **Ask the room questions** and listen to the answers; silence tells the speaker something too.
- **Move toward the room**, away from the lectern, so every section feels addressed.
- **Use flex points**: places marked in the notes where the speaker can go deeper or skip
  (`slides.md` §7).
- **Handle distractions lightly**: a remark to the whole room, never a confrontation with one
  person.

## 3. The first minute

The first minute is remembered best (`story.md` §6), and the room judges the speaker's
competence within a fraction of a second of seeing them (Willis & Todorov 2006). So:

- **Walk on, plant, look at the room, pause, then speak.** The pause before the first word reads
  as calm.
- **The first sentence is the hook**: a question, a surprising number, a short story. Not "Hi, my
  name is… and today I'm going to talk about…". The introduction can come second.
- **Promise the companion guide** within the first minute: everything is written up, the link
  comes at the end, so nobody needs to take notes.
- **Black the screen** (B) while telling a story or asking the opening question, so all eyes are
  on the speaker.

## 4. Voice ◐

- **Pause instead of filler.** A short pause at a comma, a longer one at the end of a sentence,
  and a long one, two seconds or more, after a key line so it can land. Pausing is not the same
  as speaking slowly.
- **Slow down by about a fifth at the important moments**; a speaker who is nervous speeds up.
- **Vary the voice to match the moment**: lighter and quicker for a funny aside, firm and
  clipped for a claim the speaker stands behind, slow and quiet for something personal, a
  rising-then-falling rhythm for an explanation. A whole talk in one tone loses the room.
- **End statements on a falling pitch.** Rising at the end turns a claim into a question.
- **Cut hedges.** "I think", "maybe", "kind of", "just", "to be honest" make a firm point sound
  tentative. In a recording, count them, pick the most frequent, drop that one first, then the
  next. Do this for clarity and momentum; the research does not show that hedges by themselves
  lower credibility (`concision.md`).

## 5. Eyes and body ◐

- **One person per thought.** Hold eye contact with one person for a sentence or half a
  sentence, then move to someone in another part of the room at the next pause. Each line is
  said to someone.
- **Look at the room, not the screen.** The slide is behind the speaker for the audience, not
  for the speaker. Use a confidence monitor or the laptop for a glance, then look up again.
- **Stand grounded**, weight on both feet; move with intent (walk, then plant) rather than
  pacing.
- **Watch for nervous habits** in the rehearsal recording: nodding while talking, a bouncing
  leg, fiddling with the clicker or a pen, swaying, hands in pockets.
- **Gestures follow the words** and stay open; no gesture at all is better than a repeated one.
- **Breathe out slowly** before walking on, and widen the gaze to take in the whole room rather
  than one spot; it calms the body and makes reading the room easier.

Do not repeat the claim that 55 % of meaning comes from body language. That is a misreading of
Mehrabian's studies, which were about single words spoken with conflicting tone.

## 6. When things go wrong

- **The demo fails.** Have a recording or screenshots of the working demo ready, and decide in
  advance how long to try before switching (30 seconds is plenty). Say plainly what happened and
  move to the backup; the room forgives a failed demo, not a long silent struggle.
- **The projector or laptop dies.** The talk must work without slides: the speaker knows the
  story by its images (`slides.md` §7) and keeps going while someone fixes it. Carry the deck on
  a USB stick and as an offline file.
- **The speaker loses the thread.** Pause, breathe, look at the slide or the notes, and say the
  slide's sentence again. Nobody in the room knows what was supposed to come next.
- **Time runs short.** Cut from the middle, never the close: drop the flex points first, then
  whole sections in the order planned in the brief (`talk-brief.md` §5). Always keep the next
  step and the guide's QR code.
- **A hostile or off-topic remark** in the middle of the talk: acknowledge it in one sentence,
  offer to talk afterwards, and continue.

## 7. Q&A

Questions are where the speaker shows they know the subject. Prepare for them like part of the
talk: list the ten questions the room is most likely to ask and practise the first sentence of
each answer.

**Answer first, in one sentence, then stop.** The first sentence of the answer is the answer:
"Yes, for most sites", "No, because…", "It depends on one thing: …". Then stop. If the person
wants more, they will ask, and the silence after a short answer feels much longer to the speaker
than to the room. A long wind-up before the point is the most common way to lose a Q&A
(`concision.md`).

**Pause and think before answering.** Take two to four seconds of silence to find the answer,
then speak. The pause reads as thought, not uncertainty, and it stops the speaker from thinking
aloud.

**Repeat or rephrase the question** for the room and the recording, in one short sentence. It
also buys a moment to think, and checks that the speaker understood it.

**Answer to the whole room**, not only the person who asked: start with eye contact to the
asker, then widen to everyone, and end back with the asker.

**When the speaker doesn't know:**

- Say so plainly: "I don't know." Do not guess; a guess that turns out wrong costs more than the
  honest answer.
- Then offer what the speaker does know: "What I do know is…", or the part of the question they
  can answer.
- Then offer a way forward: "Let me find out and add it to the guide", or "Does anyone in the
  room know?" Follow up as promised: the companion guide is the place for the answer.

**Other hard cases:**

- **A statement, not a question:** thank them, agree with what is true in it, and move on.
- **A question off the topic or far too detailed:** answer in one sentence and offer to continue
  afterwards.
- **A hostile question:** keep the voice level, answer the reasonable part of it, and do not
  argue; the room sides with the calm speaker.
- **Several questions at once:** answer the one that matters most, then ask which other one they
  want answered.
- **No questions:** have one ready: "A question I often get is…".

**End on the speaker's terms.** After the last question, go back to the last slide, say the one
next step again, and point to the guide's QR code. The talk ends on the next step, not on the
last question.
